\section*{1\quad Problem}
Five AI agents are asked whether a set of reports shows that a cargo vessel has left port. They split, three to two. The agents differ in how reliable they are on this kind of question, and no one knows in advance which to trust. One agent has also been compromised and argues confidently for the wrong answer. A majority vote can outvote the agents that are right, and open discussion can let the compromised agent talk the others around.

Language-model agents now routinely evaluate, critique, and debate one another's answers, and their collective judgments increasingly decide what an AI system reports. \textbf{The central problem of this project is how a group of AI agents of unknown expertise, some possibly dishonest, should weight and combine their judgments, resolve their disagreements, and improve from the outcome.}

Collective judgment goes wrong in three ways. \emph{Unknown expertise:} counting every vote equally lets less reliable agents outvote experts, and agents that make the same mistakes count more than once. \emph{Strategic and dishonest behavior:} an agent may rate its own answer too highly, rate rivals low to raise its standing, argue for an answer it does not hold, or coordinate with others. \emph{Influence without evidence:} in discussion, agents may give way to whoever is most confident or persistent. Research goal 1 (RG1) learns how much weight each agent deserves; RG2 resolves disagreements with evidence rather than persuasion; and RG3 learns from verified outcomes so the group improves. We focus on factual claims and reasoning steps that can be checked, at least in part.

\section*{2\quad Why it is important}
Using one model to judge another is now standard practice in AI evaluation and oversight \cite{zheng2023judging,verga2024juries}, and multi-agent systems increasingly decide by vote or discussion. Yet models favor their own answers \cite{zheng2023judging}, their errors are strongly correlated, so a panel of several judges may supply only a few effectively independent votes \cite{kim2025correlated,kohli2026nine}, and they abandon correct answers under pressure \cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. If agents are weighted wrongly or swayed without evidence, every evaluation and decision built on them inherits the error.

Naval information settings sharpen each part of the problem. AI assistants and automated analyses differ in reliability across tasks, and that reliability is rarely known in advance. Ground truth arrives late and sparsely, if at all. An adversary may compromise an agent or inject false reports. Checking a claim can be costly, or impossible under limited connectivity. And a human decision-maker must judge whether an AI consensus deserves trust.

\section*{3\quad Current approaches and their limitations}
\textbf{LLM judges and juries} make evaluation scalable \cite{zheng2023judging,verga2024juries}, but counting votes treats correlated errors as independent, and judges tend to favor their own answers. \textbf{Multi-agent debate} lets models critique and revise one another \cite{du2024debate}, but models also abandon correct answers under social pressure \cite{sharma2024sycophancy,laban2023flipflop}. \textbf{AI safety debate} places two debaters before a judge \cite{irving2018debate}; restricting debaters to verified quotations helps judges reach the truth \cite{khan2024debating}. These protocols assume two sides and a separate judge, and do not model the reliability or dependence of many agents.

\textbf{Reliability estimation without labels} has a long statistical history: estimating raters' error rates without ground truth \cite{dawid1979maximum}, jointly with item difficulty \cite{whitehill2009whose}, detecting copying among data sources \cite{dong2009dependence}, and allowing some dependence among classifiers \cite{jaffe2016dependent}. These methods treat raters as black boxes whose judgments never change, and infer dependence only from patterns of votes. Language-model agents instead expose their sources and reasoning, and they change one another's judgments through interaction.

\textbf{Computational social choice} offers principled guarantees for aggregation and selection. Impartial peer review ensures that a participant's ratings of others cannot change its own chance of selection \cite{kurokawa2015impartial,alon2011sum}. Related work addresses fair and manipulation-robust panel selection \cite{flanigan2021fair,flanigan2024manipulation}, when delegating votes to better-informed participants tracks the truth \cite{berinsky2025liquid}, and robust aggregation of AI evaluations \cite{khalaf2026maximal}. These mechanisms provide fairness and incentive guarantees for judgments taken as given. They do not model where judgments come from, how discussion changes them, or whether the claims being judged can be checked. We adopt their impartiality guarantees (Task 1.2) and add these elements.

\textbf{The gap:} existing methods either weight fixed judgments or let agents interact freely; none learns whom to trust from verified outcomes while protecting the group's decision from persuasion and dishonesty.

\section*{4\quad Proposed research: what is new, and why it will succeed}
\textbf{What is new.} (i) Agents are weighted by expertise learned from verified outcomes and from the reasoning and sources they expose, and agents that share mistakes are not counted twice. (ii) Aggregation weights agents by demonstrated skill yet remains impartial, so no agent can raise its own standing through its ratings of others. (iii) A debate protocol for many agents of unknown and correlated reliability lets only verifiable evidence change the answer, and agents are trained to contribute evidence rather than to win.

\textbf{Why it will succeed.} The PI's group built the components this work combines. PRD introduced peer ranking and peer discussion among language-model evaluators, achieving higher accuracy and closer agreement with human judgments than relying on the strongest single model, which favors its own answers and is sensitive to answer order \cite{li2024prd}; our PeerEval leaderboard (peereval-ai.github.io) applies peer evaluation across many current models. FG-PRM detects six types of reasoning errors at the level of individual steps \cite{li2025fgprm}. HiPRAG trains search agents with step-level rewards to retrieve only when needed, reaching 65.4\% and 67.2\% average accuracy across seven question-answering benchmarks with 3B and 7B models while cutting unnecessary searches to 2.3\% \cite{wu2026hiprag}. MEQA and the PI's event-extraction research supply tools for generating multi-hop reasoning data \cite{li2024meqa}. The PI's 2024 NSF CAREER award and 2023 Amazon Research Award on process-guided fine-tuning provide foundations for this work, and as a postdoctoral researcher the PI led system integration for the RESIN-11 system in DARPA KAIROS.

\textbf{Setting.} Each agent first answers independently, splitting its reasoning into checkable claims and citing its sources; this sealed first answer is kept as a reference. Agents then exchange messages or debate, and may revise. The unknowns are whether each claim is correct, each agent's skill on each type of task, and how agents' errors depend on one another. The information available is a few audited claims of known correctness, and checks that have a cost. The goal is accurate, calibrated decisions within a checking budget that remain robust when some agents are dishonest, and that report ``unresolved'' when the evidence is insufficient.

\rg{RG1. Learn whom to trust}
\task{Task 1.1: Expertise-aware weighting.} Majority vote gives every agent one vote. We will instead estimate each agent's expertise on each type of question and weight its judgment accordingly. Building on statistical models that estimate raters' accuracy without an answer key, in the tradition of Dawid and Skene, we will model each agent's skill per question type and each question's difficulty, informed by its record on audited claims and by the reasoning and sources it exposes. One refinement matters for language models: agents built on the same underlying model, or relying on the same sources, tend to make the same mistakes \cite{kim2025correlated}, so their agreement should not count twice. We estimate this from how often agents' errors actually coincide, not from shared sources alone: $n$ judges whose errors have average correlation $\rho$ count as about $n/(1+(n-1)\rho)$, so five judges with $\rho = 0.5$ count as fewer than two. We will measure how many audited claims are needed for reliable weights and test whether the weights recover the expertise and dependence planted in our testbeds. In the vessel example, weighting favors agents with strong records on such questions, but a confident compromised agent can still leave the decision unclear. PRD and PeerEval judgments provide baselines and data.

\task{Task 1.2: Impartial, skill-weighted aggregation.} Weighting agents by skill can conflict with impartiality. In PRD's ranking, an agent that lowers a rival's score also lowers that rival's weight as a judge, which can raise the agent's own score. Impartial mechanisms prevent this by design but weight all reviewers equally \cite{kurokawa2015impartial}. We will derive each agent's weight as a judge only from audited claims, which it can improve only by being right, so a compromised agent cannot gain influence by rating others low. Following the partition principle of impartial selection \cite{alon2011sum}, ratings will flow only between groups, and each group's outcomes will be decided by the other groups' ratings, so an agent's ratings never affect its own outcome. Agents built on the same underlying model, or sharing sources, will fall in one group, which also removes self- and family-favoring. For selecting top candidates, as on a leaderboard, we will adapt randomized impartial selection \cite{kurokawa2015impartial} and measure how much accuracy impartiality costs. Agreement within a coalition that shared evidence does not explain will be flagged as possible collusion. Language-model reviewers make it practical to test impartial mechanisms at scale, including in scientific peer review.

\rg{RG2. Resolve disagreements with evidence: check when possible, debate when not}
Both tasks follow one rule: \emph{a challenge can change the group's answer only through verifiable evidence}, meaning a record or test result obtained by a check, a quotation verified against its source, or a reasoning step reduced to one simple enough to verify directly. Confidence, persistence, and the number of voices do not count.

\task{Task 2.1: Find the disagreement that matters, and check it.} Independently produced solutions are split into claims and aligned, and the method locates where they diverge. A divergence is \emph{consequential} if reversing the disputed claim would change the final answer; step-level error detection from FG-PRM \cite{li2025fgprm} ranks the suspect steps. In the vessel example, the disagreement reduces to whether the latest port-authority entry records a departure, which can be checked directly. The method then selects a check suited to the claim: a record with independent provenance, a counterexample, or an executable verifier. Checks are prioritized by how likely they are to change the answer relative to their cost.

\task{Task 2.2: Evidence-grounded debate among many agents.} Some disputes cannot be settled by a single check: the solutions differ too much to align, or no record, tool, or connection is available to test the disputed claim directly. Even then, quotations can be verified against their sources, and arguments can be broken into steps simple enough to verify. For these disputes we will develop a debate protocol for many agents of uncertain and correlated reliability. (i) Each side is represented by its most reliable agents from RG1, and agents whose errors are strongly correlated share one speaker, so repeating a view adds no weight. (ii) Debaters question each other's arguments until the disagreement narrows to a single claim \cite{irving2018debate}; if that claim becomes checkable, Task 2.1 checks it. (iii) Only verified quotations and directly verifiable steps count as evidence \cite{khan2024debating}. (iv) A held-out panel of agents that took no side serves as judge, weighted by RG1's reliability estimates; when every agent has taken a side, a separately designated judge model decides, and no agent judges a debate involving its own answer. The outcome is an answer with reduced confidence, or ``unresolved'' with a record of what remains disputed. If the port log in the vessel example cannot be reached, the two sides debate using only quotations verifiable against the reports, so the compromised agent cannot win by assertion. To expose dishonest agents, we will track three behavioral signals, namely arguing against one's own sealed first answer without new evidence, citing evidence that does not support the claim, and changing others' answers without evidence, and feed them into RG1's weights. We will test when debate adds information beyond voting and how many dishonest debaters the protocol tolerates.

\rg{RG3. Learn from verified outcomes}
\task{Task 3.1: Close the loop.} Each dispute settled by a check becomes a new audited claim that updates RG1's estimates of skill and dependence. Because these claims are selected for being disputed, a small random sample of audited claims is maintained to keep the estimates unbiased. Outcomes decided by debate update the estimates only provisionally and never serve as audits, so persuasive errors cannot reinforce themselves. Once a check settles the vessel question, the agents that were right gain weight and the compromised agent loses standing. We will also learn which checks and debate formats resolve which kinds of dispute, and when checking is worth its cost, extending HiPRAG's rewards for searching only when needed \cite{wu2026hiprag}.

\task{Task 3.2: Train agents to contribute, not to win.} Training debaters to win, and using more persuasive debaters, can improve judge accuracy \cite{arnesen2024selfplay,khan2024debating}. These results concern two debaters and a neutral judge; with many correlated and possibly colluding agents, winning rewards persuasiveness that need not coincide with honesty. We will train agents with reinforcement learning on multi-agent and debate episodes whose rewards come from verified outcomes, building on multi-agent training of language models \cite{park2025maporl,subramaniam2025multiagent}. The difficulty is credit assignment: deciding which agent's message moved the group to a correct answer. Building on FG-PRM's step-level rewards and the PI's process-guided fine-tuning, each message will be rewarded by its effect. Introducing verified evidence that moves the group toward the correct answer, or revising one's own answer in response to such evidence, earns reward. Changing others' answers without evidence, giving way without evidence, or abandoning a correct first answer is penalized. The key experiment compares training to win with training to contribute, measuring accuracy, harmful revisions, and emergent dishonest persuasion.

\rg{Task 4 (shared). Testbeds with known expertise and dishonesty, and evaluation}
All scenarios are synthetic or drawn from public data. (A) Math and code problems solved by agents of known, varied skill, where some agents receive the same flawed hint, so expertise and dependence are known by construction. (B) Multi-document report assessment built with the question-generation pipeline behind our MEQA benchmark \cite{li2024meqa} and from the multi-hop benchmark MuSiQue \cite{trivedi2022musique}, including synthetic maritime scenarios; expertise is controlled by giving agents different documents and tools, and some reports are outdated or conflicting. (C) Scientific claims and peer review: whether a paper's evidence supports its claim, using public conference reviews and author rebuttals, with claims later confirmed or contradicted by reproducibility studies serving as audits. Agent pools will span several model families and include planted dishonest or colluding agents, created by prompting or by training. Baselines are majority vote, a single strong judge, Dawid--Skene-style weighted voting, PRD, impartial selection, plain multi-agent debate, and two-sided debate with verified quotations, all under matched compute and checking budgets. Outcomes are accuracy, calibration, how often a correct minority prevails, how often a correct answer is changed to a wrong one, recovery of the planted expertise and dependence, audits needed, the accuracy cost of impartiality, robustness to dishonest agents, and cost.

\section*{5\quad Limitations}
If every agent shares the same misconception and nothing can be checked, neither aggregation nor debate can recover the truth; our methods should recognize this and report the claim as unresolved. Checks can themselves be wrong, and debate judges can be persuaded, so we will measure sensitivity to unreliable checks and judges rather than treat either as ground truth. Robustness will be established against the dishonest and colluding behaviors we test, not against arbitrary collusion. Reliability estimates may transfer poorly to new topics or model families. Operational naval use would require domain data and expert validation beyond this fundamental research.

\section*{6\quad Expected impact}
If successful, the project will establish how to weight AI agents of unknown expertise and honesty and how much checking is needed when their judgments are not enough, together with impartial skill-weighted aggregation, a many-agent debate protocol in which only verifiable evidence changes the answer, and training that rewards contribution over winning. The naval relevance follows from the setting: agents of varying, unknown reliability (Tasks 1.1 and 3.1), late and sparse ground truth (Tasks 1.1 and 3.1), adversarial deception or a compromised agent (Tasks 1.2, 2.2, and 3.2), and costly or unavailable checks (Tasks 2.1 and 2.2). For an analyst combining reports, the resulting capability would show which agents' judgments carry weight and why, identify the claim still in dispute, and explain any change of answer or request for review. We will release code, testbeds, and evaluation protocols.
